ACL surgery: arthroscopic reconstruction and return to sport

Precise diagnosis, personalised surgery, and a return to sport guided by objective criteria — not the calendar.

What the ACL is and how it tears

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is the knee's main stabiliser against twisting. It typically tears in pivoting sports — football, padel, basketball, skiing — through a sudden change of direction with the foot planted, often without any contact. Most people notice a pop, swelling within hours, and later a feeling that the knee "gives way".

An ACL tear often doesn't come alone: meniscus, cartilage or other ligament injuries are common. Identifying and treating them in the same operation is one of the keys to a good outcome.

How it's diagnosed

Diagnosis starts in clinic with the story of the injury and specific examination tests (Lachman, pivot-shift, anterior drawer), and is confirmed with an MRI scan, which also assesses the menisci and cartilage. Your first visit with me includes a full examination and, where useful, an ultrasound scan on the spot.

Does it always need surgery?

No. The indication is individual: your age, activity, how unstable the knee feels, and associated injuries all matter. For athletes and anyone with giving-way episodes, reconstruction is usually recommended to restore stability and protect the meniscus and cartilage long-term; for lower-demand patients, structured physiotherapy can be a reasonable option. My job is to explain your options honestly and decide together with you.

The operation

ACL reconstruction is performed arthroscopically, through small incisions. The torn ligament is replaced with a graft — usually hamstring or patellar tendon, chosen for your case — anchored in the femur and tibia to reproduce the original anatomy. Where indicated, I add procedures shown to protect the result in high-demand athletes, such as meniscus repair in the same operation or an anterolateral extra-articular reinforcement in patients at higher risk of re-rupture.

The operation usually takes around an hour, with a short hospital stay in most cases, at IMED Elche or IMED Alicante hospitals.

Recovery — guide phases

Weeks 0–2

Pain and swelling control, protected weight-bearing with crutches, immediate work on range of motion and quadriceps activation.

Weeks 2–6

Progressive weaning off crutches, normal gait and full extension; basic strength work begins.

Months 2–4

Progressive strengthening, cycling, and reintroduction of running once strength and control criteria are met.

Months 4–8

Advanced strength, change of direction, jumping and sport-specific drills.

From month 8–9

Progressive return to pivoting sport once objective tests are passed (limb symmetry strength, hop tests, neuromuscular control).

These timings are a guide: every knee and every sport sets its own pace. What never changes is the method: you return when you're ready, not when the calendar says so.

Frequently asked questions

Does a torn ACL always need surgery?

Not always. It depends on age, activity, instability and associated injuries. Athletes and patients with giving-way episodes are usually advised to have reconstruction; lower-demand patients may do well with supervised conservative treatment.

Which graft will I get?

Usually hamstring tendons or patellar tendon (bone-tendon-bone); quadriceps tendon or allograft in selected cases. The choice is personalised and discussed with you, pros and cons included.

When can I play sport again?

Return to pivoting sport is decided by objective strength and control criteria; as a guide, from 8-9 months for contact/pivoting sports. Returning earlier increases the risk of re-rupture.

When can I walk and drive?

Protected walking with crutches from the first days, weaning off progressively. Driving is usually considered around 3-4 weeks. Guide times only — your recovery sets the pace.
