Knee replacement: total and partial

When arthritis has worn the knee out and pain starts making your decisions (how far you walk, how you sleep, what you give up), a knee replacement is one of the most proven operations in orthopaedics. What matters is doing it at the right time and choosing the right type.

When a knee replacement makes sense

We don't operate on an X-ray: we operate on a person. A replacement is indicated when three things coincide:

  • Advanced osteoarthritis, confirmed on standing (weight-bearing) X-rays.
  • Pain and limitation that shape your daily life: walking short distances, climbing stairs, sleeping.
  • Well-conducted non-surgical treatment that is no longer enough: exercise, weight control, pain relief and injections.

Age alone doesn't decide: the state of the knee and how you are do. Not operating too early matters as much as not waiting until the knee and its muscles have badly deteriorated. If there is still room to keep your own joint, I will tell you.

Before a replacement: keeping your own knee

My approach starts from joint preservation: a replacement comes when everything else is no longer enough. First we consider:

  • Targeted exercise, weight and load management, the foundation of any arthritis treatment.
  • Ultrasound-guided injections of hyaluronic acid or platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in mild to moderate arthritis.
  • Osteotomy: in younger, active patients with a bow-legged or knock-kneed limb and wear limited to one side of the knee, correcting the alignment offloads the damaged area and can delay or avoid a replacement.

Total or partial: how they differ

The knee has three compartments: the inner, the outer and the kneecap compartment. Depending on how many are damaged, there are two types of replacement.

Partial knee replacement

Replaces only the worn compartment, almost always the inner one. Also called unicompartmental.

  • Keeps the cruciate ligaments and the rest of your knee
  • Faster recovery; typical stay of 1–2 nights
  • Feels closer to your own knee
  • Only indicated when wear is limited to one compartment, with intact ligaments and good movement
  • On average it lasts less than a total; if arthritis progresses elsewhere in the knee, it may need converting

Total knee replacement

Replaces the surfaces of the whole knee.

  • Deals with arthritis in several compartments and marked deformity
  • On average, the longest-lasting option
  • More gradual recovery: the knee keeps improving for up to a year
  • Typical stay of 2–3 nights

Indicative summary. Timings vary between patients and the indication requires an individual assessment.

A partial replacement is not "half an operation" or a lesser implant: it is a different indication. When well indicated, it preserves more of your knee and recovery is shorter. In return, if arthritis progresses in the rest of the joint over the years, it may need converting to a total replacement. That is why the decision is made with the examination and the X-rays in front of us.

How long a knee replacement lasts

A review published in The Lancet in 2019, based on national joint registries, estimated how many replacements are still in place 25 years on:

≈ 82%total knee replacements at 25 years
≈ 70%partial knee replacements at 25 years

These are population averages. Age, weight, activity and limb alignment all influence each individual case.

What the operation involves

1 · Work-up

Weight-bearing and full-leg X-rays, blood tests and an anaesthetic assessment. If you take blood thinners or have other conditions, this is coordinated before surgery.

2 · Surgery

At IMED Elche or IMED Alicante hospital. Usually under spinal anaesthesia (numb from the waist down) with sedation, taking around an hour to an hour and a half.

3 · Hospital stay

You stand and take your first steps with help on the day of surgery or the day after. You go home when pain is controlled and you can walk safely with crutches or a frame.

4 · Follow-up

Wound care, removal of stitches or clips at around two weeks and regular reviews with X-rays.

Typical recovery

First days

Walking with crutches or a frame, ice, pain control and movement exercises from the start.

Weeks 2–6

Physiotherapy to regain bend and strength. Most people gradually come off crutches during this period.

Weeks 6–12

Daily life increasingly back to normal: longer walks, stairs, and driving once you have good control of the leg.

3–12 months

The knee keeps improving, with less swelling and more strength, for up to a year. Timings are usually shorter after a partial replacement.

Indicative timings: every patient and every knee recovers at its own pace.

What about sport?

My background is sports traumatology, so what you want to get back to matters to me. With a knee replacement you can, and should, lead an active life: walking, cycling, swimming, gym work, golf or hiking. Repeated-impact sports such as running, football or jumping are not usually recommended because they can speed up implant wear. We discuss it according to your case and your type of implant.

Risks: let's be honest

This is major surgery. Complications are uncommon, but they exist and you should know them before deciding: infection (around 1–2%), blood clots, stiffness needing extra physiotherapy or a manipulation, and loosening or wear of the implant over the years.

And something that isn't always mentioned: most patients are satisfied, but not all. In studies, up to 1 in 5 are not satisfied with the result of a total knee replacement, usually because of persisting pain or limitation. A well-chosen indication, realistic expectations and good muscle strength going into surgery help reduce that risk.

Frequently asked questions

At what age is a knee replacement done?

There is no fixed age. Most are done from around 60–65, but the decision depends on the wear, the pain and how much it limits your life, not on your date of birth. In younger patients, options that keep the joint, such as osteotomy, are considered first.

Total or partial knee replacement: which is right for me?

It depends on how many compartments of the knee are damaged and on the state of the ligaments. If the wear is limited to one compartment, the ligaments are intact and the knee still moves well, a partial replacement is an option. If arthritis affects more of the joint, a total replacement is indicated. It is decided with an examination and weight-bearing X-rays.

How long does a knee replacement last?

In national joint registries, about 82% of total and about 70% of partial knee replacements are still in place at 25 years. How long yours lasts also depends on your age, weight and activity.

How long will I be in hospital, and when will I walk?

You stand and walk with help on the day of surgery or the day after. The stay is usually 1 to 3 nights, depending on the type of replacement and your progress.

When can I drive?

Once you have good control of the leg and no longer need crutches or medication that causes drowsiness. As a guide, from about 6 weeks.

I live in Torrevieja or Orihuela Costa: where is the surgery done?

The pre-operative consultation, the planning and the follow-up visits can take place at the Torrevieja clinic (Clínica Salar), in English. The operation and the hospital stay are at IMED Elche or IMED Alicante hospital.
